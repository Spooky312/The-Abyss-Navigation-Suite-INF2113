    Timeline of lightweight SDMs evolution
    Since 1950, SDM-RCM pairing was heavyweight. In late 1970s, lightweight SDM-RCMs began
    emerging, though they retained vestiges of heavyweight. Finally, 1996 saw first truly lightweight
    RCM: User stories. Used in C3 project, it went largely unnoticed. Yet, within three years, it
    would become mainstay of lightweight projs. These are highlights of evolution of lightweight.

User story’s origin is embedded in XP practice
User stories are super-tightly bound to XP SCM.
Of original 13 practices, five (see right) are di-
rectly driving User stories. For XP teams, User
stories are written as they always have been;
effectiveness is non-sequitur.
 Customer conducts release planning,
  i.e., difficulty, cost & scheduling of
  realizing User stories.
 Real users are part of whole team for
  reqrs, priorities, and steering of proj.
 Customer defines acceptance tests for realized User
  stories; theses added into regression testing regime.
 Proj co-opts Customer’s simple evocative vision
  of how new sw works, via use of metaphor.
 Customer sets reqrs small yet with real business value, so they can be
  realized and shipped to customer frequently as "shrink-wrapped" sw features.

         Effective writing conventions: Three-C’s
    Prior to Agile conclave, XP co-creators never published their convention for writing User stories; thus
    first convention published was Connextra in May 2001. Only then did XP co-creator Ron Jeffries post
    Aug 2001 blog reaffirming stories original non-format convention, and gave it name, “Card,
    Conversation, Confirmation” or “Three-C’s”.
        Card. Story is unformatted first-hand outline, by authentic user, who stays for all three-C’s, of biz process with just
         enough info to evoke one unique proto-reqr. Story bound not by standard format, but by standard-size text box.
         (Original was 3”x5” index card.) If story too large to fit, then it must be revised to fit into smaller box; it
         encourages users to not write SRS-kind of reqrs. Story storage media can be physical or digital, as dictated by PB
         setup. Once validated, story in standard text box is used as marker in mapping its progress through realization.
        Conversation. This is collaboration of user and developer akin to Eliciting Method in RE RCM, to specify full reqr
         from ‘proto’ one and to estimate realization effort. It uses “social” eliciting techniques, exchanges documents, and
         demos prototypes. Prior to specification, proto-reqr is validated. Priority and cost notes are appended to
         validated User story, depending on available space. In parallel to specification, criteria for acceptance of sw
         realization is also generated.
        Confirmation. This is realization of sw by developer, followed by acceptance testing of said sw by authentic user
         akin to SDLC User Acceptance Testing (UAT) with two important differences: a) user already familiar with sw’s
         capability and UX because pre-coding development work is done in full collaboration with developer, and b) only
         one reqr is being tested, to allow incremental release of accepted sw to customer.

          Effective writing conventions: Connextra
     In May 2001 during their new sw installation project for UK company Connextra, Rachel
     Davies and her XP development team conceived and applied new format template for
     effectively writing User stories. Named “Connextra format”, it is sentence comprising three
     parts that are filled in by stakeholder (see below; underlined & italicized words indicate type
     of entries by stakeholders).
        As a _position , I want to _do biz process_ , so that _benefit derived .

     Tips for content style in each entry field:
         Position. Must be specific job appointment or title. Generic roles such as “user” or “manager” are
          insufficient to understand context and identify stakeholder (for conversation).
         Biz process. Work unit per breakdown of stakeholder’s job duties; not technical action such as pressing
          button. It must have measurable criteria of completion, although that is not shown with story itself.
         Benefit. Rationale or justification that is job-related, not personal. It can be any of: achieving biz goal,
          fulfilling biz purpose, or generating something of value to biz.

     Acceptance tests may be appended to User story text box, i.e., index card, data element.

            Effective writing conventions: INVEST
     Independent consultant & XP early practitioner Bill Wake published third convention of
     User story effective writing in Aug 2003 blog, “INVEST in Good Stories & SMART Tasks”.
     Acronym INVEST highlights characteristics that User stories of good quality will exhibit.
         “I” ndependent. Its content does not overlap with any others.
         “N” egotiable. Its exact representation of full reqr is completely open to being elaborated via
          conversation. (Assume “conversation” meaning as per Three-C’s.)
         “V” aluable. Its derived benefit to authentic user must be tangible & compelling.
         “E” stimable. It must have enough prioritization content to enable cost & effort derivation by orgs.
         “S” mall. It fits in given standard text box and its realization is achievable in minimum period of
          time.
         “T” estable. Its operability must be measurable, so definitive acceptance can be made of its
          suitability.
     User story authors must accompany them with scorecard showing which quality features
     are met and which aren’t. Poor scorecard doesn’t mean User story is discarded; more
     often, developers use it to critique when and in what order such User stories are processed.

              History of UseCase original version
     UseCase method is brain-child of Swedish computer scientist Ivar Jacobson, and has
     been in use for almost 60 years. From 1967, original modeling method coined
     “traffic cases” was used to specify functions and features for large telecommuni-
     cation switching systems built and operated by Ericsson. Later, for application as
     foundational element in Objectory method for specifying sw reqrs, he enhanced
     traffic cases into what we now know as “UseCases”. In particular, said UseCases
     specified and modelled all ways of using system to yield particular goal, purpose, or
     value (GPR) for particular user.
     At second annual OOPSLA in 1987, Ivar revealed concept of UseCases in his article
     “Object-oriented development in an industrial environment”, and how they were
     used at Ericsson to capture large system reqrs. In 1993, he along with Christerson,
     Jonsson & Overgaard authored book “Object-Oriented Software Engineering — A
     Use Case Driven Approach”. Among other major ideas, it introduced UseCases in
     graph & matrix representations as cornerstones of software design. After book’s
     publication, interest in applying UseCase method grew steadily.

        History of UseCase original ver (cont)
     After release of versions 0.9 and 0.91 of UML in 1996 ― being first to include Jacobson’s
     UseCases in diagram-only form ― popularity of UseCase diagrams soon eclipsed most of
     others in specification. For instance, in 2006 article “How UML is used” by Dobing and
     Parsons, usage of UseCase diagrams was tied in second place with Sequence diagrams;
     Class diagrams were first. Interestingly, UseCase matrices, a.k.a., descriptions, were in third
     place, ahead of Activity, StateMachine, and Collaboration diagrams.
     Unlike other reqrs capturing methods, UseCases can also be
     SDM, ergo, UseCase-driven 1) SDM, and 2) SWE. Its creator
     goes further: it can drive most of individual parts of whole
     SDLC (see right; even Agile SDM) as framework. E.g., Analysis
     stage utilizes UseCase value statement to generate Boundary-
     Control-Entity elements, or Architecture stage components
     are UseCases encapsulating business logic and interfaces, or
     Testing stage applies UseCase models to ascertain if newly

     Lightweight RCM applying combination of, parent-UseCase (UC) circa
     1987-92,97, Stories similar to XP User stories, and Slices that constitute
     reqrs of new not yet existing sw; subtype of parent-UC. It comprises six
     steps, 1) draw UC graphic model and list ‘Auxiliary details’ for big picture,
     2) prioritize UCs, 3) write Stories covering complete range of ways each UC
     is used, 4) bring forth via conversation full specification of reqrs behind
     brevity of Stories, 5) write Slices to align select reqrs into “vertical”
     orientation set by Jacobson’s BCE model, and 6) prioritize & deploy Slices
     for realization. Once realized, Slices are discarded, and new source code +
     UC model + Auxiliary details, become primary documentation for new sw.

           Story & Slice: Keepers of RCM’s fluidity
     Story. Short description of dialogical transactions between user and new system; in other words, a
     ‘story about way of usage’ of UC. It is authored by user or BA. UC can have several Stories covering
     all ways that usage occurs in it; crucially, all must semantically relate to GPV of their UC. There are
     three categories of usage, basic, alternate & exception. (Definitions are per parent-UC specification,
     as is one only is basic.) Finally, while UC has pre- and post-conditions, Story may not; even if it did,
     neither would be expressed by author in those terms.
     Slice. Technical specification of small portion of UC’s functionality that undergoes realization per
     lightweight norms. Said functionality is drawn from UC’s Stories, and established into an end-to-end
     vertically-oriented segment. Segment’s orientation is derived from Boundary, Control, Entity (BCE)
     elements comprising Jacobson’s Analysis Model (1992), and content from platform’s sw architecture.
     Stories are analysed for their BCE elements to determine if 1) whole Story connects through whole
     vertical stack, and if there are none, then 2) isolate parts of Stories to assemble vertical stack that fills
     out particular function. Ergo, it is usual to find that some Stories may be shared in several Slices.
     Criterion underpinning said analysis is that Slice should not be so comprehensive or complicated that
     long period of time is required to realize it.

            Prioritization scheme for UC and Slice
     UC2.0 processing model slide shows that UC from graphic model and Slices are subjected to
     prioritization. Said prioritization conducted per one of three schemes: minimum marketable
     product (MMP), minimum viable product (MVP), and Walking skeleton. Their common purpose is
     to minimizes risk and validate reqrs & assumptions early to avoid painful rewrites later, but with
     specific emphases:
         MMP. Coined by Mark Denne & Jane Cleland-Huang in 2003 “Software by Numbers: Low-Risk, High-Return
          Development”. For businesses that outsource development of product that they intend to sell or distribute.
          Must be stable and polished enough to provide reliable test market feedback while protecting brand trust.
         MVP. Coined by Frank Robinson at SyncDev in 2001, and later popularized in 2011 ”The Lean Startup”. Has
          enough features for early adopters to meaningfully use and provide feedback on what may need adjustment.
         Walking skeleton. Coined by Alistair Cockburn in 2004 “Crystal Clear: A Human-Powered Methodology for
          Small Teams”. Technical proof of concept of architecture with minimal features; backbone of development.
     Generally, Walking skeleton is realized first before either of MVP or MMP, ensuring that decisions
     pertaining to biz ops can at least have foundation of new product’s technical viability. Prioritization
     is not static; it can be redirected as more increments of shippable product are released.

       RailPocket: From PDA to Smart phone app
     Dutch Railways is principal passenger railway operator in Netherlands. To facilitate real-time
     improvement of its passenger services and experience, in February 2007 it fielded fleet of
     PDAs named RailPocket for use by its 10,000 front-line employees and 50 service
     ambassadors to do …
       Ticket validation. Conductors can read and validate passenger tickets in minutes.
       Providing updates. Employees can inform travelers in near real time with latest info on its trains and
         stations, such as schedules, onboard quantities, queuing times, and on-route station bypasses.
       Internal data entry. Conductors and engineers can immediately report problems and get action taken
         on problems that may take hours or days, such as seating capacity, train punctuality, and safety or
         maintenance issues.
     Though highly successful (and award-winning), it was bulky to carry, it was slow, its operating
     system was no longer supported, and it was operating at its capacity limit leaving no possi-
     bility of expanding its functionality. Dutch Railways launched project in 2014 to replace PDA
     with series of Railway apps powered by ubiquitous Smart phone. To conduct development
     using Agile SDM, project adopted UseCase2.0 for its RCM. Project was wildly successful!
